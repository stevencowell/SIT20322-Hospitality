WHS responsibilities

Understand how workplace duties, learner responsibilities, training, consultation and reporting combine to protect people in a hospitality environment.

01

Safety is a shared workplace system

A safe kitchen is not created by one careful worker. The organisation and people who manage or control work must provide safe systems, suitable equipment, information, training, supervision and ways to report problems. Workers and learners must take reasonable care, follow the instructions and training they have been given, use equipment correctly and avoid creating new risks for other people.

Shared responsibility does not mean equal authority. A learner may identify a damaged appliance and stop using it, while the responsible person decides how it will be isolated, assessed or repaired. Knowing the boundary of your role helps you act quickly without improvising a technical solution.

02

Follow instructions, but question unsafe uncertainty

Work instructions may come through demonstrations, signs, labels, recipes, rosters, equipment information and direct supervision. Listen, ask questions and confirm unfamiliar steps before starting. Following an instruction includes using the stated equipment, sequence and protective controls rather than only reaching the final product.

If an instruction is unclear, conflicts with approved information or appears unsafe, pause and seek clarification. Respectful confirmation is part of professional responsibility. It is safer than silently guessing, copying another student or continuing because the kitchen is busy.

03

Training and authorisation define what you may do

Hospitality workplaces contain tools, heat, electricity, chemicals and manual tasks that require correct instruction. A demonstration or online video can build understanding, but practical use still depends on the authorised local equipment, training and supervision. Never assume that watching a procedure gives permission to perform it independently.

Tell the responsible person when you are not trained, when equipment differs from the demonstration or when you need help to complete a task safely. Use the level of supervision directed for the activity and do not ask another untrained learner to take over.

WorkedExample: Observed evidence: A learner is asked to prepare equipment for a fictional kitchen task and notices that the appliance model differs from the one demonstrated in class. The controls and attachment do not match their training. Decision and reason: They decide not to experiment because familiarity with a similar appliance is not authorisation. Safe action: They leave it unpowered, keep the setup incomplete and explain the exact difference to the teacher. Result: The teacher selects an authorised alternative and adjusts the work sequence without exposing another learner. Next check or record: Before starting, the learner confirms the alternative's condition, demonstrated method and supervision requirement, then records the equipment change factually.
04

Consultation turns observations into better controls

Workers contribute to safety by sharing what they observe about equipment, layout, workload, procedures and changing conditions. In a student kitchen, this may mean raising a congestion point before production, explaining that a control is difficult to follow in the current layout or suggesting a safer sequence for teacher consideration.

Consultation is two-way. Give factual information, listen to the direction, ask what will change and confirm your role. A suggestion does not replace the authorised decision, but useful observations can reveal risks that are not visible from outside the task.

05

Report incidents, injuries and near misses promptly

If an incident, injury or near miss occurs, protect people from immediate further harm, call for the authorised assistance and report what happened. Use the local emergency, first-aid and incident procedures as directed. Do not move equipment, treat an injury or clean the scene beyond your training if doing so may create another risk or remove important information.

State what you observed, where and when it happened, who has been notified and what immediate action occurred. Do not assign blame or invent a cause. Accurate early information helps the responsible person provide support and review the control.

06

Take reasonable care during ordinary work

Everyday responsibilities include using walkways correctly, storing tools safely, keeping exits and controls accessible, managing waste, communicating before moving hot or sharp items, and avoiding behaviour that distracts or endangers others. Safety is visible in routine choices rather than only emergency responses.

Complete required checks and records honestly. A tick or signature should represent work actually performed. If a step could not be completed, report the real status and seek direction instead of creating a false record for the next worker.

Likely misconceptions

  • Avoiding delay does not justify working outside training or supervision.
  • Another learner may be completing a different task or may also misunderstand the instruction.

Support and adjustment

  • Read the purpose and success criteria aloud; keep both visible.
  • Use the labelled visual before extended reading.
  • Allow paired oral rehearsal before the saved written response.
  • Offer the scaffold without supplying an assessment answer.
  • Use the printable activity for students who need a paper pathway.
