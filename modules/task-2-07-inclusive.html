Task 2 · Lesson 7 of 8

Provide culturally respectful and disability-inclusive service

Adapt communication and access respectfully without turning cultural information, disability or customer profiles into assumptions.

SITXCOM007SITXCCS011
Teach / present this lessonOpen applied activityOpen Menu, customer and complaint cards

Prior learning

Students can ask neutral questions, confirm customer requirements and protect personal information.

Success criteria

  • Explain diversity and cultural awareness in a hospitality service context.
  • Use cultural-protocol information as a prompt for respect rather than a rule about an individual.
  • Offer assistance and communication options without taking control away from the customer.
  • Recognise an inclusive-service issue that requires authorised support or escalation.

Learning boundary

This lesson supports preparation and formative practice. The current authorised package and trainer/assessor control formal products, observations, dates and submission.

01

Diversity is normal in Australian hospitality

Customers and colleagues may differ in culture, language, religion, age, disability, family background, communication style and life experience. Cultural awareness helps workers notice that one familiar way of greeting, explaining or organising service may not work for everyone.

Inclusive service aims to give people respectful access to the same hospitality experience. It does not require a worker to know everything about every community. It requires curiosity, respectful questions, accurate information and willingness to seek support.

Practice Prompt: Describe one way a service process can be made easier to understand without singling out one group.
02

Protocols guide awareness, not predictions

The authorised diversity worksheet provides examples of cultural protocols involving respect for Elders, personal space, names and titles, physical contact, direct confrontation and permission around significant images or events. These examples show why workers should not assume their own communication habits are universal.

A protocol associated with a broad group is not proof of an individual customer's preference. Follow the customer's cues, use respectful language and ask when the answer matters to the service.

Worked Example

Unsafe Assumption: Avoid eye contact because the customer appears to belong to a particular culture.
Safer Action: Use attentive body language, follow the customer's communication cues and ask a relevant question if clarification is needed.
03

Make information easier to use

Plain language, short steps, visual references and extra processing time can make information more usable. Speak clearly rather than loudly. Avoid jargon unless you explain it. Check understanding by asking the customer to confirm the choice, not by asking a vague 'Do you understand?'

If another communication method or authorised assistance is available, offer it. Do not pretend to understand an unclear request; ask respectfully for clarification.

Before And After

  • Less Useful: That's our standard mise en place add-on.
    Clearer: That option includes the items prepared and served with the main product. Would you like me to explain each one?
  • Less Useful: You understand, right?
    Clearer: Could I confirm which option you would like me to record?
04

Ask before assisting

Disability-inclusive service starts with the customer. Speak to the customer rather than only to a companion. Ask whether assistance would be useful and how they would prefer it. Do not touch mobility equipment, move personal items or take over a task without permission.

Keep available routes and service points clear, use the accessibility arrangements provided by the workplace and seek authorised support when a request changes the normal process. The customer does not need to disclose more information than the service requires.

Source Safe Phrases

  • How can I assist?
  • Would you like the options explained in another way?
  • Is this arrangement suitable?
  • I will check the authorised option and return to you.
05

Protect dignity, choice and privacy

Inclusive service gives the customer useful choices without drawing unnecessary attention to them. Ask relevant questions quietly where appropriate and record only the information required by the authorised system.

Avoid jokes about accents, names, disability or cultural practices. Avoid speaking over the person, using childish language or assuming a companion will decide. If a mistake occurs, correct the language or action promptly and continue respectfully.

Authentic Example: A customer asks for the menu to be read aloud. The worker asks whether they would like all options or a particular section, then reads the information accurately without asking why assistance is needed.
06

When adaptation needs authorised support

A request may involve seating, service format, communication support, product information or a change to the usual process. If the worker can provide the option within their role, confirm it. If they are unsure or the request involves policy, safety or a physical change they cannot make, involve the authorised person.

Do not reject a request simply because the usual process is easier. Equally, do not promise an arrangement that has not been checked. Explain the next step and keep the customer informed.

Worked Example

Request: A customer asks for a different seating arrangement to access the table.
Response: Acknowledge the request, check the available authorised arrangement, involve the appropriate person if furniture or access routes must change, and confirm suitability with the customer.

Likely misconceptions

  • Appearance does not establish identity or individual preference. This would turn awareness into stereotyping.
  • This removes the customer's choice and assumes what assistance they need.
