Task 2 · Lesson 8 of 8

Recover service problems, manage conflict and use feedback

Use a calm recovery process for routine problems, recognise escalation limits and turn useful feedback into a documented improvement action.

SITXCOM007SITXCCS011
Teach / present this lessonOpen applied activityOpen Menu, customer and complaint cards

Prior learning

Students can record orders accurately, adapt service respectfully and identify the boundary of their authority.

Success criteria

  • Use listen, clarify, repeat, authorised solution or escalation, appropriate apology and follow-up for a routine service problem.
  • Distinguish constructive recovery from defensive or destructive conflict behaviour.
  • Identify threats, serious safety concerns, discrimination allegations or authority limits that require escalation.
  • Gather, record and use focused feedback without adding blame or unnecessary personal information.

Learning boundary

This lesson supports preparation and formative practice. The current authorised package and trainer/assessor control formal products, observations, dates and submission.

01

A six-part routine recovery sequence

The authorised common-problems worksheet supplies a six-part sequence: active listening, clarify the request, repeat the request, offer a solution, apologise if appropriate and follow up. The solution must be one the worker is authorised to provide; otherwise the correct action is escalation.

Keep the sequence calm and practical. The goal is not to prove who is right. It is to understand the service gap, protect safety, provide a legitimate next step and check the outcome.

Workflow

  • Listen without interrupting and notice the stated facts.
  • Clarify what the customer expected and what occurred.
  • Repeat your understanding so the customer can correct it.
  • Offer an authorised solution or involve the authorised person.
  • Apologise when appropriate without inventing facts or blame.
  • Follow up and record the issue through the approved process.
02

Constructive and destructive conflict

Conflict may arise around expectations, delays, product quality, communication, price, behaviour or a misunderstanding. Destructive handling can include interruption, blame, sarcasm, threats, raised voices or refusing to listen. It often increases pressure and reduces the chance of an accurate solution.

Constructive handling keeps the discussion focused on the problem and next action. Calm tone, neutral language, accurate repetition, boundaries and authorised escalation can reduce conflict even when the customer remains unhappy.

Compare

  • Destructive: 'That's not my fault; the kitchen is the problem.'
    Constructive: 'I understand the meal arrived cold. I will confirm the immediate next step with the authorised person.'
  • Destructive: Argue about whether the customer waited exactly an hour.
    Constructive: Acknowledge the reported wait, check the order status and explain the confirmed next step.
03

Know when routine recovery must stop

Routine service recovery is not suitable for every situation. Threats, harassment, violence, an immediate safety concern, a serious food-safety issue, a discrimination allegation, an unauthorised remedy or a problem the worker cannot safely resolve require the current escalation procedure.

Do not try to manage a crisis alone to prove customer-service skill. Create distance or stop the relevant service action where the authorised procedure requires it, notify the correct person and provide factual information. The exact local emergency and security response remains Teacher to confirm.

Handover Fields

  • What the customer reported
  • What was observed
  • Immediate safety action already taken
  • What the customer is requesting
  • What has and has not been promised
  • Who has been notified
04

Focused feedback is more useful than a vague check

Feedback helps a business understand needs, preferences and service gaps. It also shows customers that their experience has been heard. Sources may include conversation, a feedback form, survey, authorised digital review channel, repeated complaint, compliment or staff observation.

The question affects the quality of the answer. 'Was everything okay?' may invite only yes or no. 'Were you kept informed about the delay, and what update would have helped?' produces information that can guide an action. Ask at a suitable time and do not pressure the customer to provide a positive review.

Question Examples

  • Vague: Was it good?
    Focused: Was the order repeated accurately before it was submitted?
  • Leading: You were happy with the service, right?
    Focused: What part of the ordering process was clear, and what could be easier?
05

Record the issue, then close the improvement loop

Record feedback through the authorised process using factual language. Separate what the customer said, what was observed, the action taken and the outcome. Do not add blame, guesses about motive or unnecessary personal information.

One comment may need context; repeated comments can reveal a pattern. Improvement needs an owner, an action and a check. 'Do better' is not a plan. 'Revise the delay-update handover, practise it with the team and review feedback after the next service period' is an action that can be followed up.

Workflow

  • Collect a specific comment or observation.
  • Record facts and protect privacy.
  • Look for a service step or pattern, not a person to blame.
  • Assign or escalate an improvement action.
  • Check whether the action changed the result.
06

Worked case: delay, cold food and an undercooked burger

The authorised feedback worksheet includes a customer reporting an hour-long wait, cold food and a burger that is raw in the middle. This combines service quality and a potential food-safety concern. The immediate priority is not to debate the exact wait or offer compensation outside authority.

Listen and acknowledge the reported problems. Clarify which product is affected and ensure the food-safety concern is passed through the authorised procedure. Involve the authorised person, explain the confirmed next step, record what occurred and follow up. Any replacement, refund, discount or compensation depends on current authority.

Model Path

  • Acknowledge both the delay and the product concern.
  • Prevent further consumption or handling as directed by the authorised food-safety process.
  • Escalate with an accurate handover.
  • Do not blame another work area or promise a remedy.
  • Return with the authorised next step.
  • Ask a focused follow-up question and record the result.

Likely misconceptions

  • Explaining pressure before listening can sound defensive and does not clarify the problem.
  • Repeat-back is a normal accuracy step within routine service.
