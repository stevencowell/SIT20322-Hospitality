Task 3 · Lesson 5 of 6

Technology, trends and safer change

Evaluate current and emerging hospitality technology through workflow impact, evidence quality, customer and worker risks, fallback planning and an authorised improvement proposal.

SITHIND006
Teach / present this lessonOpen applied activityOpen Two-source industry brief

Prior learning

Recall the previous lesson and bring forward one question or completed learning artefact.

Success criteria

  • I can recognise the main technology categories named in current hospitality-industry knowledge evidence.
  • I can test whether a trend claim is current, relevant and supported rather than repeating hype.
  • I can map benefits, risks, data, people, training and fallback across a workflow change.
  • I can suggest an improvement with a clear trial, authority boundary and review measure.

Learning boundary

This lesson supports preparation and formative practice. The current authorised package and trainer/assessor control formal products, observations, dates and submission.

01

Hospitality technology supports connected systems

Current unit knowledge spans catering systems, applications for devices and computers, computer-aided despatch, food production, online booking, reservations, operations, financial and tracking systems, project management and social media. These categories describe functions; they do not require every workplace to use a particular product.

A system may connect several duties. A booking platform can affect customer information, availability, reception and reporting. A despatch or ordering system can affect order sequence, preparation and handover. A social platform can affect customer communication and reputation, while its use remains controlled by workplace authority.

AuthenticExample: A fictional venue introduces a shared event-planning system. The change affects who enters updates, how versions are identified, which staff can view customer information and what happens when access is unavailable.
02

A trend needs a source, period and scope

Technology marketing often uses words such as leading, smart, seamless or essential. These descriptions are claims, not evidence. To identify a current or emerging trend, check who reports it, what evidence period and market are covered, whether the source explains its method and whether the development affects the named hospitality role.

Official industry research can show broader patterns, while a supplier source may explain a product's features. Neither automatically proves that a local workplace should adopt it. Record what is observed, what the source reports and what remains a proposal.

Workflow

  • Write the trend claim in one testable sentence.
  • Locate a current official or method-transparent source.
  • Record the evidence period, scope and any limitation.
  • Check a second source with a different purpose.
  • Translate the finding into a role-specific question rather than an adoption decision.
03

Map the workflow before judging the tool

A technology should be evaluated against the work it changes. Map the trigger, information entered, decisions supported, handovers, customer touchpoints, records and end result. Then compare the current and proposed workflow. A faster screen is not an improvement if it creates duplicate entry, hides an allergy message or leaves no clear owner.

Look for dependencies as well as features: reliable access, staff training, correct permissions, device availability, data quality, system integration and support. The exact settings and procedures are local and must come from the authorised workplace or teacher source.

Workflow

  • Name the current task and service outcome.
  • Map who enters, checks, receives and acts on information.
  • Place the technology at the point where it changes the work.
  • Identify new dependencies, training and handovers.
  • Compare accuracy, service, workload and recovery before recommending change.
04

Balance benefit with operational and human risk

Possible benefits can include clearer information, coordination, access, consistency, speed or reduced duplication. Possible risks can include incorrect data, exclusion, reduced human checking, system failure, worker confusion, customer frustration, privacy exposure or cyber-security weakness. The relevant balance depends on the actual workflow and people affected.

Include accessibility and service judgement. A digital channel can add choice for some customers and create a barrier for others. A recommendation should preserve an alternative interaction route and identify where human confirmation remains necessary.

AuthenticExample: A fictional mobile-ordering option reduces queueing for some customers but does not remove the need for a worker who can answer questions, verify a change and support someone who cannot or does not want to use the device.
05

Customer data changes the risk question

Booking, payment, loyalty, delivery and communication systems can handle information that identifies or relates to customers. Workers should collect, view, share and store information only through authorised systems and for the work purpose. They should not copy real customer data into a classroom task, personal device or unapproved tool.

Privacy coverage and legal obligations vary, so use current OAIC information for applicability questions and official cyber-security guidance for protective practices. The operational brief can identify data types, access points and an escalation need; it must not make a legal ruling about an unknown business.

AuthenticExample: A student evaluates a fictional booking system using made-up records. They identify that names, contact details and accessibility requests require controlled access, but they refer the exact privacy obligations and retention rule to the current official and workplace sources.
06

A useful proposal includes trial, fallback and review

A technology suggestion should explain the problem, verified information, affected roles, expected benefit, main risks, training need, authorised decision maker, fallback and evidence for review. This turns a fashionable idea into a testable workplace proposal.

A small trial or simulation can reveal confusing handovers before wider use, but the workplace controls approval and implementation. Feedback should come from the people who use or are affected by the workflow. Record what changed after the trial and whether the original problem was actually reduced.

Workflow

  • Define the service problem using observable evidence.
  • Summarise the current source-supported technology option.
  • Map people, information, training, accessibility and risk.
  • Design an authorised trial and a clear fallback.
  • Choose review evidence and share the result with the responsible team.
