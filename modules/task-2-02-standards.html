Task 2 · Lesson 2 of 8

Turn service standards into observable actions

Translate broad expectations such as professional, prompt and accurate into behaviours that students can practise, observe and improve.

SITXCOM007SITXCCS011
Teach / present this lessonOpen applied activityOpen Menu, customer and complaint cards

Prior learning

Students understand the complete service cycle and the difference between internal and external customers.

Success criteria

  • Describe observable behaviours for courtesy, promptness, knowledge, accuracy, presentation, personalisation and problem resolution.
  • Explain how a policy or procedure makes a service standard repeatable.
  • Respond to a delay without hiding, blaming or making an unconfirmed promise.
  • Recognise when product knowledge ends and authorised assistance is required.

Learning boundary

This lesson supports preparation and formative practice. The current authorised package and trainer/assessor control formal products, observations, dates and submission.

01

A standard must be visible in action

Words such as professional, courteous and prompt are useful only when workers know what they look and sound like. An observable standard can be described, demonstrated and checked. Courtesy may be heard in respectful language and a calm tone. Accuracy may be seen in a complete booking record and repeated order.

Observable language also improves feedback. 'Be better with customers' is vague. 'Acknowledge arriving customers, listen without interrupting and repeat modified orders' gives a learner actions to practise.

Worked Example

Vague Standard: Provide prompt service.
Observable Version: Acknowledge the customer, communicate the next step, use confirmed timing information and provide an update if conditions change.
02

Presentation communicates before staff speak

The authorised source asks students to examine venue, staff, promotional material and food or beverage presentation. These elements create expectations. A clean, organised service point supports confidence. A menu or sign must be readable and match what staff can explain. Staff presentation should meet the current workplace requirement. Products should be presented consistently with the approved standard.

Presentation is not decoration separated from service. A damaged menu, contradictory sign or untidy handover area can create confusion and errors. If a promotional item is unavailable, workers should use current information and communicate honestly rather than letting the customer discover the mismatch later.

Practice Prompt: Identify one observable presentation standard for the venue, staff, information and product. Keep it general until the teacher supplies the local standard.
03

Courtesy and personalisation without assumptions

Courtesy includes an appropriate greeting, respectful language, attentive body language, listening and a professional close. Personalisation means responding to information the customer has provided, such as using their stated name correctly, remembering a confirmed repeat preference or adapting how options are explained.

Personalisation is not guessing from age, appearance, accent, clothing or group membership. A useful personalised response is based on a question or known record used through the authorised system.

Authentic Example: A repeat customer asks whether the same non-dairy option they previously chose is available. The worker checks current product information before confirming, rather than relying on memory.
04

Promptness includes updates

Promptness is not simply moving fast. It includes acknowledging the customer, completing the task accurately, communicating realistic timing and updating the customer when conditions change. Rushing can create errors that take longer to repair.

The booklet notes that businesses may set expected response times for greeting and delivery. The exact time is workplace controlled. Students should practise the behaviour—acknowledge, check, communicate and update—without inventing a local number.

Workflow

  • Notice the delay or change.
  • Confirm what is known through the authorised channel.
  • Acknowledge the customer and explain the current next step.
  • Avoid a time promise unless it has been confirmed.
  • Return with an update when the situation changes.
05

Knowledge and accuracy protect trust

Product knowledge helps a worker explain available options, prices, quality features and the limits of their information. Accuracy means recording the exact item, quantity, modification, table or seat and relevant request through the approved system.

Strong product knowledge includes knowing when not to answer from memory. If an allergen, ingredient, booking condition or remedy is uncertain, the correct service action is to seek authorised information. Guessing can turn a simple question into a safety or trust problem.

Authentic Example: A customer asks whether a sauce contains a specific allergen. The worker records the concern and follows the authorised information process; they do not decide from appearance or an old memory.
06

Policies make standards repeatable

Policies and procedures may cover bookings, cancellations, response times, customer data, cash, documents, keys, refunds, exchanges, complaints, food safety and escalation. They explain who may act, what must be recorded and when another person must take over.

A worker can still show empathy when a requested remedy is outside their authority. Listen, acknowledge, explain the next authorised step and involve the correct person. Do not make a promise first and seek permission later.

Worked Example

Request: A customer asks for compensation after a delay.
Source Safe Response: Acknowledge the delay, clarify the request, explain that you will involve the authorised person and pass on an accurate account.
Not Source Safe: Promise a particular discount or refund without the current policy or authority.

Formative practice

Check, read the feedback and retry

Each option explains the workplace consequence. These responses save on this device.

1.Which statement turns 'prompt service' into an observable standard?

2.A promotional board lists an item that is no longer available. What is the strongest service response?

3.A customer asks a product question and the worker is unsure. Which action demonstrates knowledge and expertise?

4.A customer requests a refund, but the worker does not know whether they are authorised. What should the worker do?

Written application

Self-check
  • Each behaviour can be observed.
  • I did not invent a local time, fee or remedy.
  • My improvement can be practised or checked.

Suggested sequence

  2. 15 min: teach the core explanation and worked example.

  4. 15–20 min: applied activity or teacher-authorised demonstration.
  5. 5 min: written response, evidence destination and exit check.

Likely misconceptions

  • Speed alone is not a complete service standard and may reduce accuracy.
  • This allows the presentation material to keep creating an inaccurate expectation.

Support and adjustment

  • Read the purpose and success criteria aloud; keep both visible.
  • Use the labelled visual before extended reading.
  • Allow paired oral rehearsal before the saved written response.
  • Offer the scaffold without supplying an assessment answer.
  • Use the printable activity for students who need a paper pathway.
