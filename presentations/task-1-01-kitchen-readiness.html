Kitchen readiness

Connect personal readiness, workplace safety, food hygiene, production planning and honest evidence before treating any one control as an isolated checklist item.

Learning intention

What success looks like

  • Explains all three parts rather than listing them
  • Shows how one missing part can weaken the whole system
  • Names a realistic control or escalation action
  • Does not invent a local procedure or assessment condition

Worked example

Control the process as conditions change

A pre-start plan is only the first version of the job. During production, monitor what is actually happening: Is a control still working? Has the bench become crowded? Has a tool been contaminated? Is the product progressing as expected? Has another person entered the work zone or changed the sequence?

Observed evidence: During a fictional cold-food practical, the pre-start was complete, but a drink spill later reached the movement path and clean tongs were placed on a used cloth. Decision and reason: The learner decides the original controls no longer protect people or food, so continuing would spread two hazards. Safe action: They pause, protect the food, warn others away from the spill, move the tongs to the designated reprocessing area and tell the teacher. Result: The authorised clean-up and equipment reset occur before work resumes. Next check or record: The learner rescans person, place, process and product, then records only the observed change and action taken.

Question the room

Which action gives the strongest start to an unfamiliar kitchen practical?

  1. Collect ingredients immediately and work out the method during production.
  2. Read the complete task, prepare yourself, inspect the area and confirm equipment before setting up.
  3. Copy the station beside you because the products appear similar.

Apply

Kitchen hazard hunt

The pictured training kitchen has been deliberately staged with eight problems before a practical begins. No equipment is operating and no person is at risk. Treat it as a visual inspection, not permission to recreate the scene.

  1. Inspect the whole photograph before selecting anything.
  2. Find the eight staged problems.
  3. For four hazards, record who or what could be harmed, the immediate safe response and who must be told.
  4. Compare with a partner and resolve one difference by referring to the lesson, not by guessing.
